Fixed Income (Bonds)

While equities dominate media attention, the bond market is vastly larger in aggregate value. It is the structural bedrock of the global economy: determining the baseline cost of borrowing for every government, corporation, and homebuyer.

Fast Structural Summary

What is a Bond at its Mechanical Core?

A bond is a standardized loan securitized into a tradable instrument. The buyer is the lender (creditor); the issuer is the borrower (sovereign treasury, municipality, or corporation). The issuer contractually promises to pay regular fixed interest payments (the coupon) and repay the original borrowed capital (the principal / par value) on a specified future calendar date (the maturity).

2. The Iron Law of Bonds: Why Price and Yield Move in Opposite Directions

When interest rates rise in the economy, existing bonds fall in price. When interest rates fall, existing bonds rise in price. This is not arbitrary; it is an unavoidable mathematical truth:

A Simple Concrete Example:

  1. In 2020, you bought a 10-Year Bond for $1,000 with a 2% coupon ($20 interest per year).
  2. In 2023, the central bank raised rates, and newly issued 10-Year Bonds now pay a 5% coupon ($50 interest per year).
  3. Now you want to sell your old bond in the secondary market. No rational investor will pay you $1,000 for a bond paying only $20 when they can buy a brand new bond paying $50.
  4. To entice a buyer, you must discount the price of your old bond below $1,000—down to approximately $780. At that cheaper price, your $20 coupon plus the eventual $1,000 return yields an effective 5% return to the buyer!
RULE: When market yield goes UP ↑, secondary bond price MUST go DOWN ↓.

3. The Two Fundamental Risks in Fixed Income

1. Interest Rate Risk (Duration)

The risk that changes in the macro benchmark interest rate will crush the bond's present value. The longer the maturity (e.g., 30-year Treasuries vs 3-month Treasury Bills), the more sensitive the bond is to rate swings.

2. Credit / Default Risk (The Spread)

The risk that the borrower runs out of money and defaults on coupons or principal. Corporate bonds must pay a Credit Spread (extra yield above sovereign debt) to compensate lenders for taking corporate bankruptcy risk.

Scientific Classification of Fixed Income Data
FACT

A sovereign government issuing debt denominated in its own non-convertible fiat currency cannot be involuntarily forced into nominal legal bankruptcy through lack of cash.

DATA

The global bond market size exceeds $130 trillion nominal value, with US Treasury securities representing over $26 trillion of marketable debt.

INTERPRETATION

An "Inverted Yield Curve" (where 2-year bonds yield more than 10-year bonds) is interpreted by macroeconomists as a strong historical harbinger of recession.

HYPOTHESIS

The Expectations Hypothesis of the Term Structure claims long-term yields equal the geometric average of expected future short-term rates. Term premia variations show limitations.
